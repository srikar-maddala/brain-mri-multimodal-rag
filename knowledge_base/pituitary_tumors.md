# Pituitary Tumors

## What is the pituitary gland
The pituitary is a small gland at the base of the brain, sitting in a bony pocket called the sella turcica just above the sinuses. It makes hormones that control growth, metabolism, reproduction and the stress response, and it regulates other glands such as the thyroid and adrenal glands.

## Types of pituitary tumors
Most pituitary tumors are adenomas, which are benign. Pituitary adenomas are common, with an estimated prevalence of about 17 percent. About 35 percent are invasive, meaning they extend into nearby structures such as the dura or the sphenoid sinus. Pituitary carcinomas are malignant and very rare, making up only 0.1 to 0.2 percent of pituitary tumors. By size, microadenomas are smaller than 10 mm and macroadenomas are 10 mm or larger; most adenomas are microadenomas.

## Functioning vs nonfunctioning pituitary tumors
Functioning tumors make extra hormones. Prolactinomas, which make prolactin, are the most common type. ACTH-producing tumors cause Cushing syndrome, growth hormone-producing tumors cause acromegaly or gigantism, and TSH-producing tumors cause hyperthyroidism. Nonfunctioning adenomas (30 to 35 percent) do not make hormones and cause symptoms by pressing on nearby structures as they grow.

## Symptoms of pituitary tumors
Vision problems are typical because a growing tumor can press on the optic chiasm above the pituitary, causing loss of side (peripheral) vision. Hormone-related symptoms depend on the tumor type: prolactinomas can cause irregular periods and sexual problems; ACTH-producing tumors cause weight gain, muscle weakness and easy bruising; growth hormone-producing tumors enlarge the hands and feet and coarsen facial features. Headaches are also common. Rarely, pituitary apoplexy, sudden bleeding into the tumor, causes severe headache, vision loss and a dangerous drop in hormones.

## How pituitary tumors are diagnosed
MRI is the imaging test of choice because it shows the pituitary in several planes with good soft tissue contrast. Blood and urine hormone tests show whether the tumor makes too much hormone or causes a deficiency. A visual field test checks for pressure on the optic nerves.

## Treatment of pituitary tumors
Surgery is often the first treatment, usually by the transsphenoidal approach, in which the surgeon reaches the pituitary through the nose and sphenoid sinus. Radiation therapy is used after surgery or when surgery is not possible, but carries about a 30 percent risk of reduced pituitary function (hypopituitarism) at 10 years. Medicines are used for some tumor types: dopamine agonists such as cabergoline and bromocriptine treat prolactinomas successfully in more than 90 percent of newly diagnosed patients, and somatostatin analogues control growth hormone and TSH-secreting tumors. Most patients do well with appropriate treatment.
