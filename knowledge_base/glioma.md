# Glioma

## What is a glioma
A glioma is a primary brain tumor, meaning it grows from the brain tissue itself. Gliomas start in glial cells, the supporting cells that nourish nerve cells and help maintain the blood-brain barrier. There are three main glial cell types: astrocytes, which transport nutrients and support neurons; oligodendrocytes, which make the insulating myelin around nerve fibers; and ependymal cells, which line the brain's ventricles and help produce cerebrospinal fluid.

## Types of glioma
The main types are astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, ependymoma and glioblastoma. Astrocytic tumors include pilocytic astrocytoma (grade 1, slow-growing and rarely spreading), diffuse astrocytoma (grade 2, slow-growing but often spreading into nearby tissue), anaplastic astrocytoma (grade 3, faster growing) and glioblastoma (grade 4, the most aggressive form).

## Glioma grades (WHO grading)
Gliomas are graded from 1 to 4 based on how abnormal the cells look and how fast they grow. Low-grade gliomas (grades 1 and 2) grow slowly and have a better outlook; grade 1 pilocytic astrocytomas mostly occur in children, while grade 2 gliomas occur in adults. High-grade gliomas (grades 3 and 4) are malignant, grow quickly and infiltrate surrounding brain tissue with tentacle-like projections, which makes complete removal difficult.

## Symptoms of glioma
Symptoms depend on where the tumor is. Common symptoms include headaches (for example a morning headache or one that eases after vomiting), seizures, nausea, weakness or numbness on one side of the body, vision or hearing problems, speech difficulties, balance problems, personality or mood changes, and unusual sleepiness.

## How glioma is diagnosed
MRI with gadolinium contrast is a primary imaging test; the contrast collects around tumor cells so they appear brighter. CT may also be used. A biopsy is needed for a definitive diagnosis: a pathologist determines the tumor type and grade. Molecular tests on the tissue look for markers such as IDH1/IDH2 mutations, 1p/19q codeletion and MGMT promoter methylation, which influence prognosis and treatment.

## Treatment of glioma
Treatment depends on grade, type and location. Some slow-growing, low-grade tumors are managed with active surveillance or surgery, with or without radiation. High-grade gliomas are treated with maximal safe surgical removal followed by radiation therapy and chemotherapy. For glioblastoma, standard treatment is surgery followed by radiation given together with temozolomide chemotherapy, then temozolomide alone. Tumor treating fields are another option. Recurrent tumors may be treated with more surgery, stereotactic radiosurgery, bevacizumab (which blocks new blood vessel growth), or clinical trials.

## Prognosis of glioma
Outcome depends on tumor type and grade, location, how much of the tumor can be removed, residual tumor after surgery, genetic changes in the tumor, and the patient's general health. CNS tumors often recur, sometimes many years after treatment, so long-term follow-up with regular MRI is needed.
