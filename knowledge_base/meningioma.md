# Meningioma

## What is a meningioma
A meningioma is a tumor that grows from the meninges, the layers of tissue that cover the brain and spinal cord. Because it grows from these coverings, a meningioma usually presses on the brain from outside rather than growing into brain tissue. Most meningiomas are not cancerous (benign), and meningioma is the most common benign brain tumor. In England, around 27 out of every 100 brain tumors diagnosed between 1995 and 2017 were meningiomas.

## Meningioma grades
Meningiomas are graded 1 to 3. Grade 1 meningiomas are slow-growing and are the most common. Grade 2 (atypical) meningiomas grow faster. Grade 3 meningiomas grow quickly but are rare. The grade depends on how the cells look under a microscope and on genetic and protein features.

## Who gets meningioma
Meningiomas are more common in women, and the risk increases with age. Previous radiotherapy to the head can increase risk, and some inherited genetic conditions, such as familial schwannomatosis, are linked to a higher risk.

## Symptoms of meningioma
Symptoms depend on location and include headaches, seizures, weakness in the arms or legs, changes in eyesight, hearing loss and loss of smell. Many meningiomas cause no symptoms and are found by chance on a scan done for another reason.

## How meningioma is diagnosed
Tests include MRI or CT scans, neurological examination, blood tests and sometimes a biopsy. These tests show the tumor's size and location and help plan treatment.

## Treatment of meningioma
Treatment depends on the grade, location, symptoms and the person's general health. Small, slow-growing meningiomas without symptoms are often managed by active monitoring with regular MRI scans, and treated only if they grow. Surgery aims to remove as much of the tumor as possible, although complete removal is not always possible when the tumor involves important nerves or blood vessels. Radiotherapy, including stereotactic radiotherapy that focuses radiation precisely on the tumor, is used for faster-growing tumors, tumors that cannot be operated on (for example at the skull base), or tumors that come back.

## Follow-up after meningioma treatment
After treatment, people have regular follow-up appointments and periodic MRI scans to check for recurrence. The frequency depends on the individual situation.
